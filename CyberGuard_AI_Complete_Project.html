1. Core Definition

Zero Trust Architecture is a cybersecurity framework based on the principle of 'never trust, always verify.' Unlike traditional security that protects the perimeter of a network, Zero Trust assumes that threats could exist both outside and inside the network at all times. Therefore, no user or device is granted access to resources until their identity and integrity are strictly verified.

2. How It Works Under the Hood

It works by requiring constant authentication and authorization for every single request to access data or applications. Instead of a single 'front door' login, the system checks the user's identity, the security status of their device, their physical location, and what specific data they are trying to reach before granting limited access.

3. Real-Life Scenario / Case Example

Imagine a high-security hotel where having a key to the front lobby doesn't automatically open any other doors. Even after you enter the building, you must scan your fingerprint to use the elevator, use your key card again to enter your specific floor, and provide a passcode to enter your room. Security guards also check your ID periodically while you are in the hallway.

4. Practical Security Applications

Zero Trust is commonly used to secure remote workers accessing company files from home, protecting sensitive cloud-based databases, managing access for third-party contractors, and preventing hackers from moving sideways through a network if they manage to break in.

5. Prevention & Mitigation Controls

Key methods include Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) to verify identity, Micro-segmentation which divides the network into small, isolated zones to contain breaches, and the Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP) which ensures users only have access to the specific tools they need for their job and nothing more.

Fundamentals

1. Introduction to Ethical Hacking

Ethical hacking involves authorized, simulated cyberattacks performed on computer systems, networks, or applications to identify and remediate security weaknesses before malicious actors exploit them.

  • ▹ Requires explicit written scope & authorization (Rules of Engagement)
  • ▹ Follows structured methodologies (PTES, OWASP, NIST SP 800-115)
  • ▹ Focuses on risk mitigation and defensive hardening
Threat Landscape

2. Types of Hackers

Security actors are categorized by intent, authorization, and adherence to legal boundaries.

  • ▹ White Hat: Authorized security professionals & penetration testers
  • ▹ Grey Hat: Operates without prior permission but without malicious intent
  • ▹ Black Hat: Malicious attackers exploiting systems for financial or destructive gain
Methodology

3. Penetration Testing Lifecycle

A five-phase structured workflow used to evaluate system resilience under controlled conditions.

  • ▹ Phase 1 & 2: Reconnaissance & Scanning/Enumeration
  • ▹ Phase 3 & 4: Controlled Exploitation & Post-Exploitation Analysis
  • ▹ Phase 5: Executive & Technical Remediation Reporting
Phase 01

4. Reconnaissance (OSINT)

Gathering publicly available intelligence about target infrastructure without triggering intrusion alarms.

  • ▹ Passive OSINT: WHOIS, DNS records, Certificate Transparency logs, Shodan
  • ▹ Active Recon: DNS zone transfers, banner grabbing, subdomain enumeration
  • ▹ Helps map the external attack surface
Phase 02

5. Vulnerability Assessment

Systematic identification, classification, and CVSS prioritization of known security flaws across assets.

  • ▹ Automated CVE scanning paired with manual false-positive verification
  • ▹ Evaluates unpatched software, weak ciphers, and misconfigurations
  • ▹ Prioritizes remediation using CVSS v3.1/v4.0 severity scores
Infrastructure

6. Network Security & Defense

Securing data in transit and protecting perimeter/internal network segments against lateral movement.

  • ▹ Next-Gen Firewalls (NGFW), Zero Trust Network Access (ZTNA), and VLAN segmentation
  • ▹ Network Intrusion Detection/Prevention Systems (NIDS/NIPS)
  • ▹ Encrypted tunneling via IPsec, WireGuard, and TLS 1.3
OWASP Top 10

7. Web Application Security

Defending modern web apps and APIs against injection, broken access control, and session hijacking.

  • ▹ Parameterized queries to eliminate SQL Injection (SQLi)
  • ▹ Context-aware output encoding & CSP headers to prevent XSS
  • ▹ Strict RBAC/ABAC checks to prevent Broken Object Level Authorization (BOLA)
Toolkit

8. Common Cybersecurity Tools

Industry-standard diagnostic and auditing utilities used in authorized security labs.

  • ▹ Nmap & Wireshark: Network discovery and deep packet inspection
  • ▹ Burp Suite & OWASP ZAP: HTTP/S intercepting proxies for web auditing
  • ▹ Metasploit & Nessus: Controlled exploit verification and vulnerability scanning
Coordination

9. Responsible Disclosure

Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) ensures vendors receive private notice and patch time before public details are released.

  • ▹ Follow organization security.txt and Bug Bounty program policies
  • ▹ Never exfiltrate sensitive user data during proof-of-concept testing
  • ▹ Allow standard 90-day remediation window prior to advisory publication
Governance

10. Legal & Ethical Frameworks

Strict compliance with national cyber laws and professional codes of ethics governs all security testing.

  • ▹ Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), India IT Act Sec. 43 & 66, UK Computer Misuse Act
  • ▹ Written Statement of Work (SoW) and Get-Out-Of-Jail authorization letters
  • ▹ Adherence to (ISC)², EC-Council, and SANS ethical codes
